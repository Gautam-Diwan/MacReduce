Spencer H. Bryngelson, Georgia Institute of Technology. The front matter calls the current edition v3 (October 14, 2026) and the original release v1 (June 21, 2026). The arXiv stamp on the first page is v2, 15 October 2026. The saved file is the PDF fetched from the URL above on the retrieval date.

The abstract says the Neural Engine is a fixed-function matrix accelerator in every Apple system-on-chip since the A11 and the M1, that applications reach it through Core ML, and that this work reverse-engineers a direct user-space route below Core ML. It says silicon was measured on the M1 and M5 only, and that each claim is marked measured, decompile-derived, or predicted. It says the route is unsupported and breaks across operating-system updates. Core ML remains the path for shipped software.

Chapter 1, as read, says the datapath multiplies in fp16 with a wide accumulator, that `computeUnits = .cpuAndNeuralEngine` is a hint because a planner still places segments, and that the private Espresso runtime compiles and dispatches with no planner in the path. The M1 envelope in table 1.3 is a compute roof of about 12 fp16 TFLOP/s, a DRAM roof of about 85 GB/s, about 0.5 pJ per FLOP sustained, a ridge near 141 FLOP/byte, and a 2 MB on-chip working set. The advertised core count and the compiler's core count are different numbers. Those M1 figures are source-reported.

Chapter 6, as read, lists five user-space steps: build a graph, compile, load, bind buffers, dispatch. The runtime is the `e5rt_*` family. One submission can hold several on-engine steps.

Chapter 14, as read, says a small or finely dispatched decoder is dispatch-bound, so int8 weights did not raise a hybrid decode rate (about 0.99 times fp16 in that measurement). At batch 16 the GPU was about 2.7 times faster and about 4.6 times more energy efficient than the engine on decode. Speculative decoding and batched prefill are source-reported serving controls, including 35 to 128 tokens per second on TinyLlama and a loaded-program cap near 128. The chapter's verdict is that encoders fit the engine and bandwidth-bound decode favors the GPU.

Chapter 15, as read, says the compiler has no engine-native backward operation. Training still runs because the host builds the backward graph from forward operations, and the optimizer state stays resident by buffer aliasing. The registered gradient set, including matmul, softmax, the normalizations, and SiLU, matched a closed form or a finite difference at cosine 1.0000. A small convolutional network reached test accuracy 0.9080 on the M1 and 0.9070 on the M5 after 300 steps. The chapter says this is a capability, not a speed claim, and that at that scale the loop is dispatch-bound. A convolution weight-gradient can saturate above width 4094 on the M1 and M2. The non-saturating slice route is described as arriving on the A15 and later. No Qwen3 or QLoRA experiment is in the pages that were read.

**What this does not establish.** It does not measure the project M4. It does not show that Qwen3-0.6B QLoRA compiles onto the engine. It does not replace the local power trace in [evidence/ane-forge](../../evidence/ane-forge/README.md).
